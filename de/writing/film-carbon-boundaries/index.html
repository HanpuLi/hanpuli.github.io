One Carbon Number Cannot Do Two Jobs

A production inventory and an avoided-emissions claim may disagree; accounting cannot decide which one should govern

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2026 essay on the political economy of greenwashing in the screen industries.

A production replaces a location shoot with an LED stage. Flights, freight and location generators disappear from the schedule. Studio electricity, rendering and data movement take their place. Its final carbon inventory can now rise even if the decision avoided emissions: the new workflow may be counted more completely than the travel-heavy alternative ever was. The opposite can happen too. A low production footprint can accompany a large claim about emissions displaced into a supplier or an infrastructure system outside the reporting boundary.

These are not versions of the same calculation. An inventory assigns emissions inside a rule. An avoided-emissions claim compares the chosen workflow with an unchosen one. The first asks what the production carries; the second what changed because a decision was made. They may share activity data, but neither total can validate the other.

Keeping two accounts is necessary, but disagreement between them creates a further problem. A stable inventory may ignore the system change that made an intervention worthwhile. A consequential model may capture that change while giving the analyst discretion over the alternative world against which success is measured. Neither method supplies a neutral rule for deciding which result should control a budget, a procurement decision or a public claim. That decision belongs to the people who set those boundaries and priorities.

A boundary creates responsibility

Carbon-accounting scholarship distinguishes attributional from consequential methods. Matthew Brander describes attributional accounting as the assignment of emissions and removals within a defined boundary. It supports inventories, targets and responsibility. Consequential accounting estimates the system-wide change caused by an action. Schrijvers and colleagues make the corresponding distinction in life-cycle assessment: an attributional LCA distributes environmental burdens across a product system; a consequential LCA models the effects of a decision.1

The screen sector needs the narrower account. A Screen New Deal, published by Arup with the BFI and BAFTA albert in 2020, assigns an average of 2,840 tonnes of CO2e to a tentpole production, defined there as a film with a budget above US$70 million. Transport accounts for about 51 per cent of the model, mains electricity and gas 34 per cent, and diesel generators 15 per cent. The estimate does not describe every film above that budget. Its value lies in giving departments categories they can see and act on.2

The 2025 albert calculator methodology shows what the resulting number is made from. Productions enter activities such as kilowatt-hours and vehicle kilometres; the calculator multiplies them by emission factors. Where direct data are absent, it uses estimates. Its LED-volume method asks for panel numbers and dimensions, then applies a benchmark developed with energy data from Garden Studios. That is a disciplined allocation procedure. It is not direct observation of every emission that enabled the production.3

A stable boundary can improve accountability by limiting the calculation. A production manager cannot wait for a model of global electricity markets, equipment manufacture and audience behaviour before choosing transport or temporary power. The same boundary also distributes visibility. What enters the calculator becomes the production’s responsibility; what sits with a supplier or in capital infrastructure may not. The total records that allocation as well as the activities inside it.

Four states are often compressed into one footprint: measured activity, such as a meter reading; estimated activity, derived from a benchmark; modelled emissions, produced by applying factors; and avoided emissions, calculated against a counterfactual. Their differences matter because data quality can move independently of physical performance.

Albert’s ACCELERATE 2025 report says that most industry data remain self-reported and unaudited. It reproduces the latest external audit’s breakdown of calculator data issues: 52 per cent estimates, 39 per cent input errors and 10 per cent aggregation errors, a rounded total of 101 per cent. It also identifies missing travel, temporary-power and waste data, retrospective collection, limited staff experience and weak integration with budgets.4

The calculator remains useful, but an improvement in its evidence need not lower the result. If one production records freight, generator fuel and temporary power that its comparator missed, its inventory may be higher because its evidence is better. Replacing estimates with invoices can also raise a total without any increase in physical emissions. A league table that rewards the lower number may then reward the thinner account.

An absolute total cannot distinguish inventory reduction from better reporting. It needs its boundary, measured and estimated shares, missing categories and relevant intensity measure beside it. Without those disclosures, the lower number may reflect fewer recorded activities rather than fewer physical emissions.

Avoided emissions depend on an unmade alternative

Studio Ulster’s 2023 report on virtual production illustrates the other account. It reports possible reductions of 20–50 per cent relative to traditional production methods, particularly for hybrid productions shooting at least 30 per cent in an LED volume. The reported saving is the difference between the workflow used and a credible alternative, not a property of the LED wall itself.5

The case study did not produce the same film twice. A four-day short made with 15 locally based cast and crew supplied recorded data for the virtual case. An experienced location manager and first assistant directors then helped construct the locations and schedule that might otherwise have been used. The researchers also modelled a hybrid scenario: 30 per cent virtual production and 70 per cent location work.6

Because a production cannot normally make the same film twice, a constructed comparison is reasonable. Its result nevertheless depends most heavily on the unobserved schedule. Different feasible locations, crew origins, construction plans or shoot lengths would generate a different saving. The 20–50 per cent range is a modelled result across deployments, not a measured property of virtual production.

Including more processes does not remove the analyst’s discretion. The report notes effects current production accounting does not fully capture, including rendering, storage, international data movement and cloud infrastructure. Regional electricity intensity can alter the balance. Panel manufacture, infrastructure utilisation and end of life raise still wider lifecycle questions. Each addition may improve the model while creating further choices about marginal suppliers, time horizons and allocation. A broader counterfactual can therefore remain contestable.

An LED stage may produce substantial reductions when it replaces long-distance travel, freight, generators and disposable construction. The advantage may shrink or reverse when it supplements location work, enables additional scenes or runs on carbon-intensive electricity. “Virtual production” names the chosen technology; the comparison still has to identify the alternative against which its benefit was calculated.

When the two accounts disagree

Imagine that the LED-stage production closes with a higher inventory than an earlier location shoot because it meters studio power and data services the earlier report omitted. Its consequential study nevertheless estimates a reduction against a credible travel-heavy schedule. One account says the production carries more emissions; the other says its decision caused fewer. Both can be methodologically sound.

Describing the first as “backward-looking” and the second as “decision-useful” assigns them different purposes without resolving the conflict. The inventory may control an organisational carbon budget because someone must carry actual emissions, even when a decision improved the wider system. The consequential result may justify the intervention because refusing it would have caused more emissions, even when the chosen production exceeds its allocated budget. Depending on which account governs, a beneficial decision can breach a target or a high-footprint production can claim success.

The GHG Protocol treats comparative impacts as a separate exercise rather than a subtraction inside a corporate or product inventory. Its separation protects against one kind of error: using an attributed total as proof of causation. It cannot prevent a favourable baseline from making an intervention look unusually effective. That requires disclosure of feasible alternatives, displaced and induced activities, the time horizon and sensitivity to the assumptions that drive the difference.7

Paired reporting should make the disagreement visible without forcing reconciliation. One page can state the inventory boundary, activity data, estimates, factors and absolute result. The other can state the decision, counterfactual, uncertainty and range of comparative outcomes. Decision makers must then say which account governs which action, and why.

Before production, comparative estimates can change locations, travel classes, construction and energy procurement. During the shoot, activity records can replace estimates while some choices remain adjustable. After wrap, the closed inventory can test the budget and improve the next production. These times and purposes should not be collapsed. Nor do they form a hierarchy in which the causal model automatically outranks the account of responsibility.

The language of a public claim should retain the language of its method: emissions are assigned inside an inventory, estimated by a model and avoided relative to a stated counterfactual. Those verbs restrain what each figure can be made to prove. They do not settle whether the organisation should prioritise lowering what it carries or changing what the wider system emits.

The choice distributes budgets, credit and obligation, so it is political in the ordinary sense. A production can claim that a decision reduced emissions relative to a disclosed alternative while still accepting responsibility for a high inventory. It can meet an internal footprint target while admitting that the chosen workflow changed little outside its boundary. Reporting both findings leaves the organisation to justify its priority in those terms.

Allocation and consequence answer different questions. Publishing both results prevents one total from standing in for the other, but may leave them pointing in opposite directions. Accounting can then describe the trade-off without choosing it. The production must state whether “better” means carrying less, causing less, or accepting a cost now to alter what can be caused later, as well as who was authorised to make that choice.
