How Praise Assigns a Film to One Author

Six texts on Joanna Hogg, three cinematographers, and the difference between directing collaboration and owning its results

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2026 independent research project in Research Methods in the Humanities and Social Sciences.

Peter Bradshaw’s 2008 review of Unrelated begins with Joanna Hogg appearing “as if from nowhere” and ends by asking what a “find” British cinema has in her. Between those phrases, he describes a film that holds its camera back, leaves voices in the middle distance and refuses to cut towards an argument occurring off screen. The observations are exact. So is the transfer that follows them. A set of formal relations becomes the evidence for a person whom criticism has discovered.1

Calling a film “Hogg’s” is not obviously mistaken. The director chooses collaborators, works across departments and may retain unusual authority over what survives into the finished film. Nor do the six texts examined here simply erase collective labour. Their more interesting operation is to admit collaboration and convert it into proof of the auteur. Different cinematographers produce films recognisable as Hogg’s; their difference becomes the experimental control against which her identity appears constant.

That operation forces a distinction the singular name usually suppresses. Organising, authorising and selecting other people’s work are forms of authorship. They are not the same claim as originating the properties that work produces. The auteur survives this distinction, but in altered form: not as the hand behind every shot, nor merely as a convenient label, but as the person whose power to arrange collaboration is retrospectively read as the source of its results. Hogg is a revealing case because a collaborator describes the work in almost exactly those terms and still calls it “very much an auteur film”. The contradiction is not between auteurism and collaboration. It runs through the auteur itself.

The evidence is deliberately small: three Guardian reviews by Bradshaw and three Sight and Sound texts, later republished in BFI Southbank programme notes, on Unrelated (2007), Exhibition (2013) and The Souvenir (2019). Catherine Wheatley, Andrew Tracy and Kate Stables wrote the three magazine pieces; the BFI selected them but did not author their sentences. Half the corpus comes from one critic, all six texts are admiring, and they cannot stand for Hogg’s reception as a whole. They are enough to show how praise assigns agency inside these particular sentences.

A discovery with a lineage

Ciara Barrett has already shown that early criticism repeatedly made Hogg legible through an overwhelmingly male European auteur canon: Ozu, Rohmer, Haneke, Bresson and Antonioni. The six-text sample does not rediscover that lineage. It shows that comparison has a direction, and that direction helps decide who explains whom.2

Wheatley’s paragraph on Unrelated first distinguishes Hogg from the “working-class realism” of Lynne Ramsay and Amma Asante, then places the film beside the “pared-down narratives and painterly compositions” of François Ozon and Éric Rohmer. Every name is complimentary, but the names do different jobs. The women mark the British cinema from which Hogg departs; the men provide the aesthetic company she joins. Because Asante is a Black British filmmaker, gender cannot explain the arrangement alone. Race, national cinema and the critical coding of social realism are also at work. The local fact is still sharp: distance is established through Ramsay and Asante, affiliation through Ozon and Rohmer.3

Bradshaw later calls The Souvenir a contemporary English “response” to Antonioni and Visconti. “Response” gives Hogg agency—she is not an imitation—but fixes the order of the conversation. The older men speak first; her cinema answers. William Brown’s sustained study of Hogg and Ozu shows another direction. He uses the relation to think about sparseness and slowness in both filmmakers. His comparison narrows the charge: hierarchy enters when the canonical name remains the explanatory source and the living filmmaker becomes its new instance.4

The same asymmetry governs “discovery”. Bradshaw’s first review makes Hogg both agent and object: she made the film, but criticism makes her appear. Six years later he again hears sound in the “middle-distance” in Exhibition. The recurrence may describe a real formal continuity. It also records the continuity of his own instrument. A critical vocabulary repeated across films helps stabilise the author it then reports as stable.5

“No matter what cinematographer”

The most revealing sentence arrives in Bradshaw’s review of The Souvenir. He identifies a look Hogg has contrived across her work “no matter what cinematographer she is working with”. This is not omission. The cinematographer enters the sentence in order to be made variable. Oliver Curtis shot Unrelated, Ed Rutherford Exhibition, and David Raedeker The Souvenir. If the look persists while that credit changes, Hogg appears as the invariant cause.6

The inference is plausible but incomplete. Production credits prove participation, not the origin of a shot; three different names do not by themselves disprove directorial control. Yet continuity across changing crews can support at least two causal accounts. Hogg may specify the relevant properties and instruct others to realise them. Or she may establish a process in which different collaborators, responding to related constraints, produce family resemblances that no one participant could have specified in advance. The films alone do not tell us which account is right.

That uncertainty matters because a director’s authority is real. She can hire, refuse, redirect and select. An egalitarian picture of a crew in which every contribution has equal force would conceal the hierarchy it claims to correct. Berys Gaut’s account of multiple authorship allows several people to author a film while exercising different kinds and degrees of control. Hogg plainly matters. The live question is what causal work her name is doing each time a critic moves from a visible feature to “Hogg’s style”.7

“A collaboration, but … an auteur film”

Raedeker’s account of making The Souvenir supplies the process evidence the reviews lack. He describes discussions across departments, shared decisions about camera formats, lighting and movement, and shot suggestions from production designer Stéphane Collonge. Hogg, he says, gives people freedom while retaining control. Then he offers what looks like a verdict: “It is a collaboration, but it is very much an auteur film.”8

An anti-auteur reading could treat the second clause as an ideological residue and keep the first. That would repeat the allocation it objects to, accepting testimony only when the collaborator speaks the analyst’s preferred language. Raedeker experienced collaboration and Hogg’s authorship together. His account therefore rules out the simple claim that the auteur label is just a critical fiction imposed against production reality.

But it also makes origin harder to assign. When he explains the mixed formats that materially structure the film’s look, his subject is “we”: “we came up with” the solution. Hogg’s control may consist partly in producing the conditions under which such a pronoun can act—choosing people, setting limits, recognising an idea and deciding to keep it. That is substantial authorship. It is relational authorship, dependent on contributions whose exact outcome control cannot fully pre-contain.

Raedeker’s account rules out a simple anti-auteur thesis. Auteur criticism does not have to deny collaboration. It can absorb collaboration by treating the director’s power over its conditions as ownership of what emerges. The move catches something true—Hogg’s unusual span of authority—while sliding between two propositions: she organised the process; therefore the film’s properties originated with her. Only the first follows from her position.

The pronoun a review needs

A short review cannot reconstruct the production of every frame. It needs subjects for its verbs, and a director’s name lets it connect formal decisions across films made years apart. “Hogg’s framing” may be the least cumbersome way to discuss a body of work. Trouble begins when that metonymy hardens into a production fact.

The six texts make three versions of that hardening visible. Discovery turns the critic’s encounter into the arrival of a coherent author. Directional comparison lets established auteurs explain the newcomer without being revised by her. Variation among collaborators supplies evidence that the director alone persists. None of these operations makes the reviews’ formal perceptions false. They determine where those perceptions come to rest.

There is no clean replacement. Naming every collaborator would not establish who made a particular decision, and replacing “Hogg” with “the crew” would erase authority as efficiently as auteurism erases dependence. The better sentence must sometimes remain uncertain: directorial authorship may name the power to sustain a field of decisions rather than the power to originate each one.

That leaves criticism with an awkward possibility. “Hogg’s look” may be most accurate when it is least literal—when the name identifies a durable way of arranging other people’s agency. Yet the possessive makes that relation sound like property. Raedeker can hold the two together because he worked inside them: “an auteur film”, and also “we came up with”. A review that keeps only the first pronoun does more than shorten the story. It changes what kind of author the film is allowed to have.
