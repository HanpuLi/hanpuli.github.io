The Listener Inside the Accent Score

Why a score of one voice also measures who is listening—and what they are allowed to demand

Hanpu Li

Two groups hear the same recording. Both recover the words. One rates the speech easy to understand; the other reports effort and a strong accent. If the groups repeat their judgements consistently, the difference is not necessarily noise. It may be the object the score has captured: not a property sealed inside the speaker, but a relation between this speech and these listeners.

That answer solves one measurement error and exposes a harder one. Once the listener is inside the construct, a score can be reliable and practically predictive while preserving the listener’s prejudice. It may accurately forecast that employers, examiners or strangers will penalise a voice. Accuracy does not turn the penalty into a speech defect. Yet refusing to help a speaker who has chosen accent modification can leave that person to bear the penalty unaided.

An accent profile therefore cannot become ethical simply by separating its columns. Accentedness, comprehensibility and intelligibility must be distinguished; the task, comparison variety and listener population must be named. The recommendation must then expose its direction: change the speaker, change the listening conditions, change the listeners, or distribute the work among them. Otherwise a decision about who should adapt disappears into the word “need”.

Three scores can describe the same voice

Munro and Derwing’s 1995 experiment established a distinction that pronunciation work still has to defend. Accentedness was listeners’ rating of difference from a target variety. Comprehensibility was rated ease or difficulty of understanding. Intelligibility was operationalised as the words listeners transcribed correctly. Other studies use other tasks, so the operation has to travel with the label.

The measures correlate, but not enough to stand in for one another. Some strongly accented speech in the experiment remained highly intelligible. A listener can recover every word while noticing distance from a familiar prestige form, or while working harder than the transcript score reveals. Conversely, vocabulary, syntax and context can impede understanding even when pronunciation attracts little comment.

Kazuya Saito’s 2021 meta-analyses make the practical difference sharper. Across 37 listener studies, comprehensibility judgements related to segmental, prosodic and temporal features, while accentedness was especially tied to segmental accuracy. Across 17 instructional studies, gains were larger for comprehensibility than for nativelikeness. Training directed at “reducing accent” may therefore spend effort on the most noticeable distance rather than the feature most consequential for understanding.

A phonetic transcription cannot decide the consequence in advance. It records an analyst’s account of a token relative to a comparison system. It does not show that a listener missed a word, used more effort or assigned a social identity. Those are separate observations. The familiar profile becomes misleading at the moment a difference on the page is allowed to inherit all three.

Reliability can preserve the wrong thing

Rater agreement matters. A result that changes randomly between listeners cannot support much inference. But agreement answers only whether listeners behave consistently. It does not establish what their consistency represents.

Stephanie Lindemann and Nicholas Subtirelu review evidence that listeners can be “reliably biased”. Shared expectations about a speaker’s linguistic or social group may produce stable judgements not reducible to the acoustic signal. In that situation a highly reliable instrument has not overcome listener variation. It has measured a common listening norm with precision.

The reverse conclusion would be too easy. Listener effects do not make every rating infinitely subjective. Talia Isaacs and Ron Thomson compared 20 experienced and 20 novice raters, assigned to five- or nine-point scales, judging 38 speakers for comprehensibility, accentedness and fluency. They found high internal consistency and no group differences in mean ratings by experience or scale length, although rating strategy and use of the nine-point scale’s middle differed. Professional and novice listeners do not have to disagree for the listener to remain part of the measure.

This counterexample changes the claim. An accent score is not relational because listener groups will always yield different means. It is relational because the procedure defines whose perception counts, even when another sampled group happens to agree. A score produced by unfamiliar monolingual listeners and one produced by multilingual colleagues may converge. They still answer questions about different populations.

The distinction becomes uncomfortable when the intended population is discriminatory. If a study samples the actual gatekeepers a speaker must face, their ratings may have strong predictive validity for a job interview or oral examination. The score can then be socially accurate and linguistically unjust at the same time. Construct validity cannot tell the speaker which fact matters more.

Accommodation has a direction

Gass and Varonis showed that comprehension of non-native speech can improve with familiarity—with the topic, non-native speech generally, a particular accent or an individual speaker. Topic familiarity was especially influential in their study. The same recording can therefore support different performance without any change in its acoustic content.

That is not automatically a measurement failure. A workplace may need to know what happens on a first call with an unfamiliar client and what happens after regular colleagues have learned one another’s speech. The mistake is to average those encounters into a speaker property. Familiarity belongs to the causal account: it identifies a route to easier communication that does not require the speaker to approximate a prestige variety.

Speaker-side intervention remains one route among others. Pacing, emphasis, prosody and repair can matter for comprehensibility. A person may also want a less socially marked accent for reasons that have nothing to do with word recovery. John Levis’s distinction between nativeness and intelligibility principles clarifies the choice but cannot purify it. The actor preparing a role, the candidate facing a gatekeeping assessment and the worker anticipating discrimination may all rationally choose different targets.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association treats accent modification as elective and distinguishes an accent from a communication disorder. That distinction prevents one ethical wrong: medicalising linguistic difference. It does not make every “choice” equally free. A service can be voluntary while employment, assessment or repeated social penalty makes refusal costly.

Respect for agency therefore pulls in two directions. Declining a requested goal because it responds to prejudice can deny the speaker a useful strategy. Accepting the goal without naming the prejudice can convert a social penalty into a personal deficit. “The learner wants it” and “the listener requires it” describe different sources of demand, even when they lead to the same exercise.

The person cannot be recovered from the sample

Reading, word lists, picture description and conversation distribute demands differently. A passage fixes vocabulary and syntax, encourages monitoring and removes lexical choice. A word list isolates contrasts while stripping away prediction and interaction. Spontaneous speech adds planning, hesitation and repair. Conversation adds another person who can infer, interrupt and accommodate.

A meticulous transcript can establish how someone read those items on that occasion. It cannot silently expand into a portrait of how that person speaks English. Repeating a practised list after instruction is not strong evidence of transfer; performance can improve through familiarity with the items. If the practical concern is a telephone call, face-to-face speech with visual cues is a different condition. If the concern is workplace repair, isolated word transcription observes only one part of the event.

A defensible profile can state its goal, task, comparison variety, listener population and outcome. It can keep word recovery apart from perceived effort and perceived accent. It can sample controlled items, connected speech and interaction where the intended use demands them. More than one rater and transcriber can reveal disagreement; a new, related task can test whether practice transferred.

Those improvements replace “the accent” with conditional claims: these listeners recovered these words from these samples; these listeners reported this effort; this feature increased distance from this declared variety. They also allow interventions on more than one side—speaker strategies, clearer task design, listener exposure, opportunities for repair. A report becomes more useful as it stops pretending that one person contains the whole problem.

But the columns can still conflict. A feature may leave intelligibility intact, increase perceived accent and predict a real social penalty. The analyst can describe all three honestly. No validity coefficient determines whether the priority should be resisting the penalty, helping the speaker evade it, or doing both under conditions that cannot make the burden equal.

The listener inside the score is therefore more than a methodological nuisance to be controlled. The listener may be a communication partner, a source of accommodation, a gatekeeper or all three at once. If the analyst removes that person from the account, prejudice reappears as a defect in the speaker. If the analyst simply treats the listener’s response as the target outcome, prejudice acquires the authority of data. A responsible profile must name the listener population, the purpose of the score and the bearer of every recommended change. Speaker-side work may be offered as a strategy under pressure; it must not be recorded as proof that the pressure originated in the speaker.
