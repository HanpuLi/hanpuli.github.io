Poetry
Voucher

A poem, issued on receipt paper.

Poems written before this project are offered by the piece, with a base specification and optional extras. Poetry Voucher places individual writing inside the familiar routines of a supermarket purchase.

Enter the shopRead the poems

01

Why a shop

Poems are slow, personal writing: they take time, attention and knowledge, and they are not interchangeable. The shop does the opposite. It treats each poem as a unit on a shelf, gives it a base specification and lets a till calculate a price. The project began after I found a receipt printer and tried printing my poems on it. The supermarket receipt became a way to ask what changes when private writing is issued through the routines of an ordinary commodity.

02

How the price works

The fictional tariff does not measure writing time, difficulty or literary value. It reduces the source text to countable features: characters, non-empty lines and stanzas, then moves the base amount up to a retail .99 price point. Pixel type is the included specification; website typefaces are sold as an upgrade. Each added translation costs more because it adds paper and printing and because a translation is another cultural form of the work. No money is taken.

03

Receipt, voucher, paper

An order is issued as one continuous 58 mm strip: receipt first, then CUT HERE, then the numbered poem voucher or vouchers. The receipt records the configured transaction; the voucher lets the writing leave that transaction. Cutting is offered, not required. Thermal paper keeps the scale and handling of an everyday receipt: it fits in a pocket, can be handed to someone else and will eventually fade. Fading is a material condition, not yet a timed claim made by the work. Visually, the edition keeps the density, hierarchy and bluntness of a till receipt rather than cleaning the form into a boutique label.

04

Digital access, physical form

The browser shop is the public distribution route while there is no exhibition venue. It generates one continuous PDF and a complete offline-readable text counterpart without taking payment or sending the poem to a server. Physical test prints have also been made on the receipt printer. The intended material encounter remains the narrow thermal strip; the browser makes that edition accessible beyond a physical site.

05

Critical frame

The work does not claim that receipt printing makes poetry new, or that its tariff fairly models creative labour. Its tension is more specific: writing that is personal and non-interchangeable is temporarily handled as stock, configuration and price. The retail system can count textual units, paper and options, but cannot account for the time, knowledge, attachment or relation that made the poem. What happens after the transaction remains with the reader: keep it, cut it, give it away or discard it.

06

Related practices

The project sits near artist publishing, take-away editions and rule-based language systems, but does not treat resemblance as a genealogy. Relevant comparisons include Marcel Broodthaers’s Pense-Bête, Felix Gonzalez-Torres’s paper stacks, Short Édition’s literary dispensers and Alison Knowles and James Tenney’s The House of Dust. The useful question is not whether receipt-paper literature is unprecedented, but what this particular retail rule does to these particular poems.
