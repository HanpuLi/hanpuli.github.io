How Final e Made Different Histories Look Alike

A nine-lexel PLAEME study of a spelling pattern that regularised more than one vowel history

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2021 squib and a 2022 History of English paper; the corpus study and argument are new to this version.

The modern forms name and time can illustrate the same school rule: a vowel followed by one consonant and final e is read with its long value. As a reading convention, the comparison is useful. As an account of how the vowels became long, it joins histories that have to be separated. Old English nama had a short stressed vowel and a final vowel; tīma already had a long stressed vowel. The final letter shared by their modern descendants cannot establish one common lengthening event.1

The problem is larger than correcting one etymology. A spelling can help predict present pronunciation while retaining an uncertain relation to the earlier pronunciation from which it developed. To examine that relation, this essay uses nine contrastively selected lexels in the Parsed Linguistic Atlas of Early Middle English (PLAEME). The query asks whether final e separates inputs associated with Middle English lengthening from inputs whose long vowels have another history. It does not assume that the written letter records a pronounced syllable.

The inputs and the limits of the query

PLAEME adds syntactic annotation and searchable lexels to diplomatic transcriptions from the Linguistic Atlas of Early Middle English. The working release used here comprises 68 parsed files and 172,624 words dated 1250–1325. These are corpus units rather than 68 independent authors or texts: files represent scribal-language material, and a source can be divided across more than one file. Lexels generally serve a lemma-like purpose without being identical to modern dictionary headwords.2

Four of the selected modern-English lexels have short-vowel disyllabic sources relevant to accounts of Middle English lengthening: name, nose, hope and tale. The comparison group contains time, home, stone, life and wife. Bosworth–Toller records inherited length in tīma, hām, stān, līf and wīf; the last two also provide monosyllabic Old English inputs. Selection is thus by historical contrast, not by random sampling of words that happen to end in e today.3

The query searches all 68 parsed files for these exact lexels, retaining singular common nouns tagged N or N-RH and excluding explicit genitives and plurals. A token counts as final-e when its diplomatic word form ends in e after removal of corpus control marks for morpheme boundaries, insertions, line breaks and abbreviations. The spelling itself is retained: tyme counts, tim does not. This avoids conflating unrelated words through a modern-string search, but it can miss material indexed under another historical lexel. The totals belong to this query at a fixed commit, not to the whole population of Middle English noun forms.4

An association that does not identify a history

The selected short-vowel group takes final e in 160 of 213 retained tokens; the inherited-long group does so in 260 of 691. The difference is visible in this sample, but its aggregate conceals an important overlap.

Time has final e in almost the same proportion as name, despite already having a long vowel in Old English. Home and stone behave differently from both; life and wife also take the letter in part of the record. The distribution therefore cannot be read as a unique tag for a vowel lengthened through the loss of a following syllable. The high time count is a direct counterexample to that interpretation, rather than an inconvenient exception to be averaged away.

Variation occurs within files as well as between lexels. Sixteen of the 34 files containing life have both final-e and non-final-e forms, as do seven of the 18 containing wife. Smaller mixed counts occur for six other selected lexels. These are file-level diagnostics, with some files sharing a scribal source. They establish that a source can vary within a lexel; they do not estimate the prevalence of a dialect or the number of independent writers making that choice.5

LAEME’s documentation supplies a reason not to translate every written ending into sound: some final es lack etymological justification and may be decorative. The category cannot be assigned mechanically to the present tokens. It establishes a possibility that their letter alone cannot exclude. Copying practice, analogy, abbreviation and scribal preference can maintain a form whose relation to a final spoken vowel is uncertain.6

The counts support an association within the selected words, not a statistical finding that the language’s distribution was non-random. Frequencies are unequal, tokens are clustered in sources, and the lexical selection was contrastive. What survives those restrictions is the failure of a one-to-one interpretation: historically different inputs share the ending, while individual sources can alternate its presence. The query does not date the modern reading rule, but it establishes why the rule cannot simply be projected back onto these written forms.

What a spelling can do for a reader

The modern agreement of name and time consequently needs to be explained at another level. Their orthography places them in a useful relation between a vowel graph, a following consonant and a non-syllabic final letter, without sorting them by the origin of vowel length. This is one limit of treating spelling as an archive: the record is also a convention used and altered by readers and writers. A form can remain available because of what it helps a reader do, even when that use no longer reproduces the conditions from which the form arose.

Wiley and colleagues’ English Sublexical Toolkit measures graded spelling–sound consistency across more than 13,000 present-day words. Their preference for final E over silent E makes the functional point: an unpronounced letter can still supply information. The toolkit does not establish the historical route from Middle English forms to modern regularity. It helps distinguish that historical question from the present task for which words such as name, same and tame can support one another as reading models.7

Metre constrains the same distinction within a line. Harvard’s Chaucer materials discuss “hire browe broune, hire eye blake” from Alysoun: six occurrences ending in e, with hire repeated, hence five distinct written forms. Pronouncing every final e as a syllable would overfill the line. The analysis supports syllabic endings in browe and eye, but not in hire, broune or blake. The written ending is the same while its prosodic work differs. Metre limits the possible reading in this witness; elision, dialect and editorial mediation keep it from becoming a direct recording of speech.8

The causal dispute requires other evidence

The vowel in name still presents a causal question the spelling count cannot settle. Middle English Open Syllable Lengthening treats a short stressed vowel in an open syllable as the relevant environment; Kim’s account makes lengthening conditional on the following vowel’s weakening and sensitive to dialect and chronology. Minkova and Lefkowitz instead propose Middle English Compensatory Lengthening, in which final-schwa deletion preserves prosodic weight by lengthening the stressed vowel. These are competing accounts of the relation between the two changes, not alternative labels for an event already agreed upon.9

A written final e cannot choose between them. It is compatible with a pronounced ending, an ending being lost, an orthographic retention after loss, or an analogical addition. PLAEME’s overlapping lexels and mixed files reinforce that underdetermination, but they do not favour one causal theory simply by showing that the letter is unreliable. The observation concerns orthography; the competing explanations concern sound change.

A further study would need to compare dated and localised sources, distinguish source-level variation, test alternative lexel assignments and bring in rhyme or metre where available. Later corpora would be needed to establish when heterogeneous endings became the more regular modern convention. Until those steps are taken, the nine-lexel count has a definite but limited result: final e crosses the historical division it was asked to identify.

Name and time can therefore remain neighbours in a reading lesson while being separated in an explanation of vowel length. The useful convention does not need one common cause to do its present work. Historical analysis does need to recover the different causes, and the final letter alone cannot supply them.
