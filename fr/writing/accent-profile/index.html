The Listener Inside the Accent Score

Why a score of one voice also measures who is listening and what they are allowed to demand

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2022 learner-profile assignment. No learner recording, transcription or biographical detail is reproduced.

Two groups hear the same recording. Both recover the words. One rates the speech easy to understand; the other reports effort and a strong accent. If the groups repeat their judgements consistently, the difference is not necessarily noise. It may be the object the score has captured: not a property sealed inside the speaker, but a relation between this speech and these listeners.

Placing the listener inside the construct corrects one measurement error but creates an ethical problem. A score can be reliable and practically predictive while preserving the listener’s prejudice. It may accurately forecast that employers, examiners or strangers will penalise a voice, but accuracy does not turn the penalty into a speech defect. Refusing to help a speaker who has chosen accent modification can nevertheless leave that person to bear the penalty unaided.

An ethical accent profile needs more than separate columns. It must distinguish accentedness, comprehensibility and intelligibility, and name the task, comparison variety and listener population. Any recommendation should also say who is expected to change: the speaker, the listeners, the conditions in which they meet, or some combination of them. Without that information, the word “need” conceals a decision about who should adapt.

Three scores can describe the same voice

Munro and Derwing’s 1995 experiment established a distinction that pronunciation work still has to defend. Accentedness was listeners’ rating of difference from a target variety. Comprehensibility was rated ease or difficulty of understanding. Intelligibility was operationalised as the words listeners transcribed correctly. Other studies use other tasks, so the operation has to travel with the label.1

The measures correlate, but not enough to stand in for one another. Some strongly accented speech in the experiment remained highly intelligible. A listener can recover every word while noticing distance from a familiar prestige form, or while working harder than the transcript score reveals. Conversely, vocabulary, syntax and context can impede understanding even when pronunciation attracts little comment.

Kazuya Saito’s 2021 meta-analyses make the practical difference sharper. Across 37 listener studies, comprehensibility judgements related to segmental, prosodic and temporal features, while accentedness was especially tied to segmental accuracy. Across 17 instructional studies, gains were larger for comprehensibility than for nativelikeness. Training directed at “reducing accent” may therefore spend effort on the most noticeable distance rather than the feature most consequential for understanding.2

A phonetic transcription records an analyst’s account of a token relative to a comparison system. It cannot show by itself that a listener missed a word, used more effort or assigned a social identity; those require separate observations. A profile becomes misleading when it allows a difference on the page to stand in for all three.

Reliability can preserve the wrong thing

Rater agreement tells us whether listeners behave consistently, which matters if a result is to support inference. It does not identify what produced that consistency or what the shared judgement represents.

Stephanie Lindemann and Nicholas Subtirelu review evidence that listeners can be “reliably biased”. Shared expectations about a speaker’s linguistic or social group may produce stable judgements not reducible to the acoustic signal. In that situation a highly reliable instrument has not overcome listener variation. It has measured a common listening norm with precision.3

Listener effects do not make every rating infinitely subjective. Talia Isaacs and Ron Thomson compared 20 experienced and 20 novice raters, assigned to five- or nine-point scales, judging 38 speakers for comprehensibility, accentedness and fluency. They found high internal consistency and no group differences in mean ratings by experience or scale length, although rating strategy and use of the nine-point scale’s middle differed. The listener remains part of the measure even when professional and novice groups produce similar means.4

Here, “relational” describes how the procedure decides whose perception counts; it does not predict that listener groups will always yield different means. Scores from unfamiliar monolingual listeners and multilingual colleagues may converge while still answering questions about different populations.

Predictive validity and linguistic justice can separate when the intended listener population is discriminatory. Ratings from the actual gatekeepers a speaker must face may predict performance in a job interview or oral examination. That social accuracy does not make the resulting penalty linguistically just, and construct validity cannot decide which consideration the speaker should prioritise.

Accommodation has a direction

Gass and Varonis showed that comprehension of non-native speech can improve with familiarity: with the topic, non-native speech generally, a particular accent or an individual speaker. Topic familiarity was especially influential in their study. The same recording can therefore support different performance without any change in its acoustic content.5

Familiarity need not be treated as a measurement failure. A workplace may need to know what happens on a first call with an unfamiliar client and what happens after regular colleagues have learned one another’s speech. Averaging those encounters into a speaker property would erase the difference. Familiarity belongs to the causal account because it identifies a route to easier communication that does not require the speaker to approximate a prestige variety.

Speaker-side intervention remains one route among others. Pacing, emphasis, prosody and repair can matter for comprehensibility. A person may also want a less socially marked accent for reasons that have nothing to do with word recovery. John Levis’s distinction between nativeness and intelligibility principles clarifies the choice but cannot purify it. The actor preparing a role, the candidate facing a gatekeeping assessment and the worker anticipating discrimination may all rationally choose different targets.6

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association treats accent modification as elective and distinguishes an accent from a communication disorder. This keeps linguistic difference from being medicalised, but “elective” says little about the conditions under which someone chooses the service. Employment, assessment or repeated social penalty can make refusal costly.7

A requested goal may respond to prejudice and still represent a speaker’s considered choice. Declining it can deny the speaker a useful strategy; accepting it without naming the prejudice can convert a social penalty into a personal deficit. “The learner wants it” and “the listener requires it” describe different sources of demand, even when they lead to the same exercise.

The person cannot be recovered from the sample

Reading, word lists, picture description and conversation distribute demands differently. A passage fixes vocabulary and syntax, encourages monitoring and removes lexical choice. A word list isolates contrasts while stripping away prediction and interaction. Spontaneous speech adds planning, hesitation and repair. Conversation adds another person who can infer, interrupt and accommodate.

A meticulous transcript can establish how someone read those items on that occasion. It cannot silently expand into a portrait of how that person speaks English. Repeating a practised list after instruction is not strong evidence of transfer; performance can improve through familiarity with the items. If the practical concern is a telephone call, face-to-face speech with visual cues is a different condition. If the concern is workplace repair, isolated word transcription observes only one part of the event.

A defensible profile should state its goal, task, comparison variety, listener population and outcome. Word recovery must remain separate from perceived effort and perceived accent. Where the intended use requires it, the sample can include controlled items, connected speech and interaction. More than one rater and transcriber can reveal disagreement; a new, related task can test whether practice transferred.

These changes turn “the accent” into several conditional findings: these listeners recovered these words from these samples; these listeners reported this effort; this feature increased distance from this declared variety. Intervention can then address speaker strategies, task design, listener exposure or opportunities for repair. The report no longer assigns the whole communication problem to one person.

Some results will still conflict. A feature may leave intelligibility intact, increase perceived accent and predict a real social penalty. The analyst can describe all three, but no validity coefficient determines whether to resist the penalty, help the speaker evade it, or attempt both without pretending that the burden is equal.

The listener is part of what an accent score measures. A communication partner may accommodate a speaker and still act as a gatekeeper. Removing that person from the account turns a social penalty into a defect in the speaker; treating the listener’s response as the sole outcome gives the penalty the authority of data. A responsible profile must name the listener population, the purpose of the score and the person expected to change. Speaker-side work can be offered as a strategy for dealing with pressure without being recorded as evidence that the pressure originated in the speaker.
