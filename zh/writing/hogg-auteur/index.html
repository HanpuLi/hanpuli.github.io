How Praise Assigns a Film to One Author

Joanna Hogg’s critical reception, the construction of authorship, and the gendered terms of its discovery

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2026 independent research project.

Peter Bradshaw’s 2008 review of Unrelated introduces Joanna Hogg as appearing “as if from nowhere” and closes by celebrating a “find” for British cinema. Between those formulations, his descriptions of camera distance, off-screen action and voices are specific enough to identify what the film does. The difficulty begins with the relation between the observations and the discovery: formal features are attributed to Hogg, gathered into a coherent practice, and then offered as evidence of an author whose coherence appears to have preceded the critic’s work.1

The critical act has to do two things at once. It constructs an authorial position through selection and attribution, while presenting that position as something the film has allowed it to discover. This does not make Hogg’s work unreal or her directorial decisions fictitious. It asks what else must happen before a decision becomes evidence of an individual style, and before that style acquires the authority of an established aesthetic practice.

The original research behind this essay concerned that double operation and its gendered forms. Its evidence was six texts: Bradshaw’s three Guardian reviews and magazine pieces by Catherine Wheatley, Andrew Tracy and Kate Stables on Unrelated (2007), Exhibition (2013) and The Souvenir (2019). Quotations from the magazine pieces use the BFI Southbank programme versions republished in 2023, which may be edited or abridged. The critical bylines and the BFI’s selection have distinct roles in their circulation. Half the corpus comes from one admiring reviewer, so recurrence within it cannot establish a consensus across Hogg’s reception.

What the sample can establish is the movement within particular sentences: from describing form to assigning it, and from assignment to a lineage that makes it evaluable. Collaboration tests one part of that movement. Gendered comparison tests another. Neither can be settled by asking whether Hogg is really an auteur, because a verdict about her competence would leave the procedures through which competence becomes authorial evidence untouched.

A discovery with a lineage

Ciara Barrett has already examined how criticism makes Hogg legible through a predominantly male art-cinema canon. The present sample does not discover the existence of those comparisons. It asks how they work at the level of a paragraph: which filmmaker supplies the established terms, which is explained through them, and whether the comparison revises both sides or leaves one as the standard.2

Wheatley’s account of Unrelated places Hogg at a distance from the “working-class realism” of Lynne Ramsay and Amma Asante, then affiliates the film with the “pared-down narratives and painterly compositions” associated with Ozon and Rohmer. These are two different operations. The women identify a British practice from which the film departs; the men supply aesthetic company in which it can be recognised. The passage does not explicitly rank Ramsay and Asante as lesser filmmakers. Its grammar establishes distance and affiliation, from which a prestige hierarchy may be inferred only with further attention to how social realism and art cinema are valued. Asante’s position as a Black British filmmaker also prevents gender from serving as the sole explanation.3

Bradshaw’s later description of The Souvenir as a “response” to Antonioni and Visconti gives Hogg an active place in the conversation, but the conversation has an order: the earlier work supplies the terms to which the new film responds. Comparison need not always confer prestige in that direction. It could put the established work under pressure too, asking whether Hogg’s use of space makes a familiar account of the earlier filmmaker insufficient. That possibility is an alternative task for criticism, not evidence that every comparison in this sample already performs it. The question is whether the older filmmaker remains an instrument for recognising the newcomer, or whether the newcomer also changes what can be seen in the older work.4

Tracy’s comparisons make the geographical description matter. Alongside Haneke and Antonioni, his Exhibition text invokes Hong Sangsoo and Corneliu Porumboiu. The cited filmmakers are men, but the set is not exclusively European. Moreover, Tracy uses the comparison to warn against equating fictional artists’ statements with their directors’ practices. A male comparator can therefore do more than lend prestige: it can help set a limit to biographical interpretation. Calling every comparison the same legitimating gesture would remove a difference the analysis needs to explain.

The recurrence of a formal term requires similar care. Bradshaw places sound at a middle distance in both his 2008 and 2014 reviews. That may identify a persistent feature of the films; it also identifies a persistent resource in his writing. Six years of recurrence within one critic’s practice cannot by itself prove that a convention has become institutional consensus. It shows the point where a descriptive instrument helps stabilise the author it then reports as stable.5

Continuity and its attribution

Bradshaw’s Souvenir review makes the relation unusually explicit. The film’s look persists “no matter what cinematographer she is working with”. Oliver Curtis shot Unrelated, Ed Rutherford Exhibition, and David Raedeker The Souvenir. The collaborators are acknowledged, then made the changing terms against which Hogg appears constant. The sentence does not merely omit labour. It gives that labour a role in establishing continuity as hers.6

The inference is plausible, but continuity does not identify the origin of each feature. Hogg may specify a property and require others to realise it; she may also sustain a working process in which related constraints yield results no participant specified in advance. A finished film cannot reconstruct the difference. Credits establish participation without telling us who proposed, altered or retained a particular shot, while recurring properties can establish a practice without proving that one person originated all its results.

Gaut’s account of multiple authorship matters here because it need not imagine an egalitarian crew to recognise several authors (Gaut, 2010). Hiring, refusing and selecting give a director substantial authority; collaborators may nevertheless contribute decisions that are authorial rather than merely executive. The problem is not solved by replacing Hogg with a list of names. It concerns the kinds of agency gathered under her name, and the additional claim made when control of a process becomes possession of everything that process produces.7

Process, collaboration and the finished work

Raedeker’s account of The Souvenir supplies testimony about that process. He describes cross-department discussions, decisions about formats, light and movement, and shot suggestions from production designer Stéphane Collonge. Hogg grants freedom while retaining control. His own formulation holds the two experiences together: “It is a collaboration, but it is very much an auteur film.” An analysis that kept only the collaboration would disregard the collaborator at precisely the moment he complicates its preferred conclusion.8

The mixed-format decision makes the complication material. Raedeker describes what “we came up with”, assigning the work to a plural subject without claiming that authority was equally distributed. Hogg’s authorship may include choosing collaborators and sustaining conditions in which their proposals can become part of the film. That is an account of directorial agency with consequences for the finished image; it leaves open which outcomes came from instructions and which emerged through the agency those instructions made possible.

Tracy’s Exhibition text recognises a related tension within the work. It follows the film’s interest in artistic process, including D’s desire for continuing improvisation, then calls the completed film “polished, purposeful and meticulously constructed”. His account is conscious of the irony. Openness within the subject does not mean that the object has no construction, and an artist’s fictional declaration cannot be treated as a production record. The critic therefore sees process without surrendering the judgement of purposeful form. That makes his text more difficult than a simple instance of collective work disappearing beneath an author’s name.9

Stables provides the strongest counterexample to an account of uniform erasure. Her Souvenir text moves from Hogg’s architectural practice to Collonge’s production design and Raedeker’s wide shots, before assigning the selective force of close-ups to Hogg. These are differentiated attributions inside one account. They do not establish the production history of every decision, but they do keep particular collaborators visible as agents of particular properties. The director remains the career-spanning subject without becoming the only grammatical subject of the film’s form.10

The six texts consequently do not perform one identical conversion. Bradshaw’s changing cinematographers strengthen an attribution to Hogg; Stables can retain the attribution while locating some work elsewhere; Tracy recognises a tension between process and completed form. Their differences narrow the claim: criticism constructs continuity through attributions that can absorb collaboration, but absorbing it does not always require denying who did the work.

The critical account is part of the problem

Stables’s closing paragraph also puts the gendered reading under pressure. She judges Hogg’s film through “Anthony’s most thoughtful film edict”, with the finished work realising an aesthetic proposition voiced by a fictional man whose conduct the review has already described as coercive. The proposition can function as a male anchor for female achievement. It can also be read as deliberate irony: the film achieves a form that exceeds the man who professes it, and the critic recognises that reversal. Both readings have to remain available because the sentence occurs inside an account of Julie’s artistic development rather than as an unqualified endorsement of Anthony.11

The ironic reading changes what the evidence can prove. A self-aware citation of a man does not establish that male anchoring is a structural necessity no criticism could escape. It establishes that the terms of male authority can remain usable within a critique of that authority. Whether the citation restores his priority or makes his inadequacy visible depends on the work done by the surrounding account. Declaring that its effect must be the same whatever the intent would turn a real interpretive alternative into a device for protecting the thesis.

The more defensible claim concerns how praise becomes evidence. Discovery language makes a critic’s construction appear as an encounter with a pre-existing author; comparison supplies established terms through which the encounter can be valued; attribution gathers the work into a practice associated with a name. Within this small corpus, men repeatedly supply comparative terms, but the sample cannot establish whether that pattern is specific to women’s reception, to the selected critics, or to auteur criticism more generally. A male director’s reception and hostile or ambivalent reviews would put those alternatives under pressure.

This essay also makes Hogg’s practice legible through selected authorities and a selected set of sentences. Its comparison with Barrett and its appeal to Gaut give the interpretation a lineage, while its decision to centre gender makes some attributional procedures easier to see than others. That dependence is not equivalent to every operation it criticises. It does mean that exposing the construction of authorship cannot give the analysis a position outside the construction of intellectual authority.

The object that remains is therefore the attribution itself: what a sentence allows a collaborator to do, what it reserves for the director, and which prior work supplies the terms in which the result becomes serious art. Hogg’s decisions do not cease to exist when their critical representation is questioned. What ceases to be innocent is the passage from observing a film to announcing that one has found its author.
