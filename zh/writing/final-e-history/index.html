How Final e Made Different Histories Look Alike

A nine-lexel PLAEME study of a spelling pattern that regularised more than one vowel history

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2021 squib and a 2022 History of English paper; the corpus study and argument are new to this version.

Name and time now teach the same lesson. In both words, a vowel followed by one consonant and final e receives what schoolbooks call its long value. The lesson works. It also makes the two words look as if they arrived together.

They did not. Old English nama had a short stressed vowel and a final vowel; tīma already had a long stressed vowel. The first word belongs to a disputed Middle English history of lengthening and final-vowel loss. The second did not need that event to acquire length. Their present agreement is not a remnant of one shared origin. It is the achievement of a later spelling system that learned to treat unlike histories as the same reading problem.1

A two-word contrast can expose the mistake, but not tell us whether it is typical. I therefore tested a small, fixed sample against the Parsed Linguistic Atlas of Early Middle English (PLAEME). The result is neither that final e meant nothing nor that it transparently recorded vowel history. It was patterned, unstable and already capable of crossing the historical distinction a modern reader is tempted to recover from it.

The sample was fixed before the count

PLAEME is built from diplomatic transcriptions in the Linguistic Atlas of Early Middle English. Its working release contains 68 files and 172,624 words dated 1250–1325. The unit needs care: a LAEME file represents a scribal language, not necessarily one literary text, manuscript or independent speaker. PLAEME preserves the manuscript detail of LAEME while adding syntactic annotation and searchable “lexels”, labels that usually work like lemmas but are not identical to them.2

Before counting, I selected nine modern-English lexels. Four have short-vowel disyllabic sources relevant to accounts of Middle English lengthening: name, nose, hope and tale. Five reach the modern final-e pattern from inherited long vowels or other structures: time, home, stone, life and wife. Bosworth–Toller records the long vowels directly in tīma, hām, stān, līf and wīf. The sample is contrastive, not a random draw from the lexicon.3

I searched all 68 parsed files for those exact lexels, retaining singular common nouns tagged N or N-RH and excluding explicit genitives and plurals. A token counted as final-e when its diplomatic word form ended in e after removing only corpus control marks for morpheme boundaries, insertions, line breaks and abbreviations. I did not modernise the spelling: tyme counted, tim did not. The method avoids a modern-string search that would merge unrelated forms, but exact lexels can miss tokens indexed under another historical label. The totals describe this query and this release, not population prevalence.4

The division appears, then fails to hold

The short-vowel group favours final e. The query returned name with final e in 107 of 132 tokens, nose in 3 of 5, hope in 14 of 26 and tale in 36 of 50. Across these selected lexels, that is 160 of 213 tokens. The inherited-long group uses final e less often overall: 260 of 691 tokens.

It is not a historical label. Time, with its inherited long vowel, takes final e in 130 of 169 tokens—almost the same proportion as name. Home has it in only 5 of 70, and stone in 9 of 60. The monosyllabic life is split 92 to 186; wife, 24 to 90. If the letter uniquely marked a vowel newly lengthened through loss of a following syllable, time should not sit beside name, while life and wife should have little reason to enter the pattern at all.

The instability is not only between words or regions. Sixteen of the 34 PLAEME files containing the lexel life use both final-e and non-final-e forms. Seven of the 18 files containing wife do the same. Smaller mixed counts occur for name, hope, tale, time and stone. A scribal source can therefore vary within one lexel; aggregating a file into a single regional form would destroy evidence rather than summarise it.5

LAEME’s own documentation warns that some final es are not etymologically justified and may be ornamental or “decorative”. That category cannot be mechanically assigned to each token in this sample. It does show why presence alone is weak evidence for a pronounced final vowel. Orthography had resources of its own: copying practice, analogy, abbreviation, metre and scribal preference could preserve or supply a letter after its relation to speech had become uncertain.6

The counts thus refuse two simple stories. Final e was not randomly scattered; the selected short-vowel inputs take it more often. Nor was it a faithful tag attached to one phonological history; the categories overlap, and individual scribal files mix forms. The later reading convention did not emerge from pure noise. It regularised a distribution that already contained both historical signal and historical ambiguity.

Name and time become neighbours

This changes the familiar description of spelling as an archive. An archive is often imagined as a store in which an old form waits to be decoded. The PLAEME forms show something more active. Written conventions preserve, vary and regroup. By the time modern readers encounter name and time, the orthography no longer sorts words by the origin of vowel length. It sorts them by a present relation between a vowel graph, an intervening consonant and a non-syllabic final letter.

Psycholinguistic evidence explains why the regrouping is useful. Robert Wiley and colleagues’ English Sublexical Toolkit measures graded spelling–sound consistency across more than 13,000 words. They prefer “final E” to “silent E” because an unpronounced letter can still carry information. Readers learn distributions across families of words; name, same and tame can support a pronunciation for a new string without sharing every stage of etymology.7

The modern pattern is therefore not a degraded historical record. It is a different organisation of the evidence. Its success depends on suppressing distinctions a historian needs. Name and time become useful neighbours when their separate routes cease to matter for reading. The final letter predicts something precisely because the system has made provenance secondary.

Metre shows the same divergence at a smaller scale. Harvard’s Chaucer materials discuss the line “hire browe broune, hire eye blake” from Alysoun. Every content string is written with final e, but pronouncing all five as syllables overfills the line. The metre supports syllabic endings in browe and eye, not in hire, broune and blake. Written identity coexists with prosodic difference inside one line. Even there, metre constrains rather than records speech: elision, dialect and editorial mediation remain possible.8

The sound change needs different evidence

Name contains a live disagreement about why its stressed vowel became long. The traditional account, Middle English Open Syllable Lengthening (MEOSL), treats a short stressed vowel in an open syllable as the relevant environment. Myungsook Kim’s analysis makes the change conditional on weakening of the following vowel and sensitive to dialect and chronology. Donka Minkova and Michael Lefkowitz propose Middle English Compensatory Lengthening (MECL): deletion of final schwa preserves prosodic weight by lengthening the stressed vowel.9

The disagreement is causal, not terminological. In one account syllable structure conditions lengthening; in the other length compensates for deletion. The written final e cannot select between them, because the same surface letter is compatible with a pronounced ending, an ending in loss, a retained spelling and later analogy. The PLAEME count strengthens that warning. It shows the letter crossing into lexels whose long vowels have another history while remaining variable in the very lexels for which lengthening is at issue.

Nor can the count itself decide the dispute. These are orthographic tokens from a restricted period, selected through nine lexels with unequal frequencies and uneven regional representation. They do not measure vowel duration. A larger study would stratify dates and localisations, model source-level variation, test alternative lexel assignments and compare rhyme or metre where available. Later corpora would be needed to trace when the heterogeneous forms became the more regular modern convention.

The school rule survives, but its history has changed. Final e is useful not because it faithfully carries one ancient event into the present. It is useful because English spelling eventually made several events readable in the same way. The letter did not merely outlive a sound. It joined words that sound history must pull apart again—and the better it performed that new job, the less it could tell us how each word got there.
