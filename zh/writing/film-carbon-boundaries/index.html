One Carbon Number Cannot Do Two Jobs

Inventories, counterfactual savings, and the passage from a technical benefit to a claim of sustainability

Hanpu Li

Developed from a 2026 essay on the political economy of greenwashing in the screen industries; the accounting analysis extends that earlier work.

Suppose a production replaces a location shoot with an LED stage. Flights, freight and generators are removed from the proposed schedule, while studio electricity, rendering and data services enter it. A claim that the change reduces emissions needs a comparison with the feasible shoot it replaced. A claim about the production’s footprint needs an inventory of the activities assigned to it. The same decision can support both accounts, but the accounts do not establish the same thing.

The distinction matters to the political economy of greenwashing because a benefit established for one decision can be made to stand for the responsibility of an entire production, or even for the legitimacy of further expansion. An inventory allocates emissions within a boundary; an avoided-emissions estimate asks what would have happened under another course of action. If the latter is allowed to cancel the former, a high-emitting production can turn its own hypothetical alternative into a reason why its actual burden no longer needs to be faced.

That possibility does not invalidate virtual production or make every carbon account a promotional device. It requires the analysis to distinguish a genuine technical benefit from the further claims made in its name. Better measurement can change an inventory without changing physical performance; a credible comparative saving can coexist with a high absolute total. The question is how the passage between those findings distributes responsibility, and who gains authority to make it.

What the inventory makes visible

Brander’s distinction between attributional and consequential accounting supplies the necessary starting point. Attributional accounting assigns emissions and removals within a defined boundary and supports inventories and targets; consequential accounting estimates the system-wide change caused by an action. Schaubroeck and colleagues distinguish the corresponding tasks in life-cycle assessment. The vocabularies overlap, but the important separation here is between distributing burdens across a product system and modelling the consequences of choosing it.1

A Screen New Deal, published by Arup with the BFI and BAFTA albert in 2020, shows why an allocated account remains useful. Its model assigns an average of 2,840 tonnes of CO2e to a tentpole production, defined in the report as a film budgeted above US$70 million. Transport contributes about 51 per cent, mains electricity and gas 34 per cent, and diesel generators 15 per cent. These proportions identify departments and activities on which action can be taken. They do not measure every film above the threshold, and cannot be transferred to another production without examining its activities.2

The albert calculator’s 2025 methodology makes the construction of a footprint inspectable. Productions supply activity data, such as electricity consumption and vehicle kilometres, to which emission factors are applied. Where activity has not been measured, benchmarks can supply estimates. Its LED-volume method, for example, takes panel numbers and dimensions and uses a benchmark developed with Garden Studios’ energy data. The resulting figure is an estimate produced by an allocation procedure, even where some of its inputs have been measured directly.3

The distinction between measured activity and modelled emissions is not a reason to reject the calculator. It is a reason to keep the stages visible. A meter reading, a benchmark for an unmetered activity, an emissions factor and a saving against an unchosen schedule have different evidential statuses. Compressing them into one footprint can conceal where a production supplied data and where the calculation supplied an assumption.

ACCELERATE 2025 reports that much industry data remain self-reported and unaudited. It gives the latest external audit’s breakdown of calculator data issues as 52 per cent estimates, 39 per cent input errors and 10 per cent aggregation errors. Those are categories of issues identified in the audited sample, not an error rate for the whole industry; the reported percentages total 101 per cent. The report also names missing travel, temporary-power and waste data, retrospective collection and weak integration with budgets.4

An apparent improvement in a footprint can consequently have more than one cause. A production that records freight and generator fuel omitted by another may return a higher total while furnishing better evidence. Conversely, a lower result may reflect a real reduction, a narrower boundary or a thinner record. An emissions league table cannot tell these possibilities apart from the total alone. The boundary, estimated shares and missing categories are part of what the number means.

Keeping the boundary stable can make responsibility more enforceable: departments can compare records and budgets without changing the rule whenever a result is inconvenient. That same stability can leave effects with suppliers or capital infrastructure rather than with the production. The allocation is therefore useful and consequential in its own right. It determines what an organisation has undertaken to count, not everything that has changed because it acted.

The alternative production has not been made

Studio Ulster’s 2023 report illustrates the comparative task. It identifies possible virtual-production reductions of 20–50 per cent against traditional methods, particularly in hybrid productions using an LED volume for at least 30 per cent of the shoot. The finding concerns deployments and alternatives; it cannot be attached to the technology as a fixed saving wherever an LED wall appears.5

The study did not make one film by both methods. A four-day short with 15 locally based cast and crew supplied recorded data for the virtual case. An experienced location manager and first assistant directors helped construct the location schedule that could otherwise have been used; a further scenario modelled 30 per cent virtual and 70 per cent location production. The unmade shoot is informed by professional knowledge, but it remains the unobserved side of the comparison.6

That is a reasonable way to investigate a decision whose alternative cannot normally be produced for experiment. It also locates the result’s principal sensitivity. Feasible locations, crew origins, set construction and shoot length determine what is avoided. Choosing an expensive, distant alternative can make the same LED workflow look more beneficial than comparison with a local, restrained one. Neither alternative should be selected merely because it gives the desired difference.

Widening the model does not abolish these choices. The report identifies rendering, storage, international data movement and cloud infrastructure as effects not fully captured by current production accounting. Electricity intensity, panel manufacture, utilisation and end of life raise further questions about which activities the decision induces and how their burdens are assigned. A broader model may be more informative while remaining dependent on marginal suppliers, time horizons and a disputed baseline.

A virtual workflow can save emissions by replacing travel, freight, generators and disposable construction. If it instead supplements location work or enables additional production, the relevant comparison changes. That is a possible rebound pathway, not evidence that every efficiency gain has already been cancelled. Establishing the aggregate result would require evidence about actual production volumes and displaced or induced activity. A benefit per shoot does not establish a reduction across the industry.

A saving cannot cancel an obligation

The conflict can be stated without treating unlike totals as contradictory measurements. Imagine that a production’s complete inventory is higher than an earlier, less complete location report, while its comparative study estimates a saving against a credible travel-heavy schedule. The inventory records what is assigned to the chosen production; the comparison estimates what changed relative to that schedule. Both findings may be sound. They become misleading when either is used to certify the other, or when the reported saving is treated as a deduction from the actual inventory.

The GHG Protocol’s product standard separates inventories from comparative claims. Russell’s 2019 WRI working paper goes further in the corporate context: reporting scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions and setting science-based reduction targets should take priority, and comparative assessments should not detract from that work. The two accounts therefore do not arrive with equal priority under this guidance. Avoided emissions are an additional claim, not a credit with which an organisation can discharge its inventory responsibilities.7

This priority does not make a comparative study irrelevant to procurement. A decision may increase emissions assigned to one organisation while reducing the wider effect against its feasible alternative. The organisation still has to carry the assigned emissions and explain how the decision relates to its target. A comparative model can inform that explanation only if it exposes the alternative, the activities displaced and induced, the time horizon and the assumptions that control the result.

Paired reporting should make those obligations inspectable. The inventory needs its boundary, activity data, estimates, factors and absolute result. The comparative account needs the decision, feasible baseline and sensitivity range. Before the shoot, these can change travel, construction and energy choices; after wrap, they can show whether the organisation has met the commitments it made. Keeping the accounts separate also prevents a favourable counterfactual from silently changing what the production agreed to count.

The remaining conflict is political rather than an arithmetic failure. A lower-carbon method may make an expansion cheaper or easier to defend without establishing that expansion itself reduces emissions. An organisation can accurately report an improvement per production while its absolute burden rises with the number of productions. Evidence of that rise would be needed to make the empirical claim; the methodological point is that the improvement alone cannot settle it.

The public language should retain the calculation’s object. An inventory assigns a burden within stated rules; a model estimates an effect; a decision avoids emissions relative to a disclosed alternative. To move from any of these to an unrestricted claim of sustainable filmmaking requires further evidence about the activities and responsibilities the claim now covers. The question raised by greenwashing is at that passage: how a limited result becomes permission for a larger course of action, and how the burdens left outside it are still to be answered for.
