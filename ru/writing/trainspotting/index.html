From Gears to Gasp

Trainspotting and the body’s investment in its own subjection

Hanpu Li

This public version has been revised for the web.

Danny Boyle’s Trainspotting (1996) opens with Renton running while his voice lists the things a life is supposed to contain. “Choose life. Choose a job. Choose a career.” The speech makes its refusal unmistakable, but the terms in which it refuses are less easily separated from what it rejects: the market supplies both the goods and the available language of wanting them. This essay follows that dependence from the voiceover into the body, through the withdrawal bedroom and finally into Renton’s theft. Its claim is that the film brings a tradition of working-class self-possession to a point where the self can no longer be securely distinguished from the forces it opposes.

The tradition is a point of departure rather than a continuous history in miniature. In Chaplin’s Modern Times (1936), the machinery that disciplines the worker is visibly outside him; in Karel Reisz’s Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960), Arthur Seaton defends an inner life against the factory while defining that life through private appetite. British Social Realism made working-class characters and their environments central to its account of social experience (Street, 2009, pp. 91, 94). Hill (1986) questioned the political consequences of locating resistance inside the individual. Trainspotting inherits that problem, but its voice, rooms and bodies make the boundary on which private resistance depends increasingly difficult to sustain.

1. Voiceover and the grammar of self-management

Seaton’s defiance presupposes that the worker has something the factory has not taken: a first-person position from which to identify its demands and refuse them. Yet the life he reserves for himself is a good time outside work, with other claims dismissed as propaganda. The factory does not determine every pleasure he takes. The difficulty is that the freedom he defends remains intelligible as the freedom of a privately satisfied individual, rather than as a claim to change the conditions he and other workers share.

Foucault’s account of governmentality helps identify this difficulty without making Seaton a neoliberal subject before his time (Foucault, 1979). Rule can operate through the conduct of people who understand themselves to be acting freely; an order from outside is not its only form. The application here concerns the terms of Seaton’s self-description. His private republic may resist a foreman while leaving intact an organisation of freedom through individual appetite. The foreman need not move literally into his head for the distinction between autonomy and accommodation to become unstable.

Renton’s catalogue makes the instability audible. The opening cuts between the street and the running body while the voice accumulates commodities, domestic arrangements and instructions for an acceptable future. Refusal does not interrupt the sequence long enough to establish another vocabulary. The items continue to determine what counts as a possible life, even as the speaker rejects them. The delivery can register fatigue with that language and fluency in it at once; neither hearing removes the dependence on the catalogue.

The claim is therefore narrower than saying that Renton means the opposite of what he says. Irony can reject a demand without escaping its terms. Nor does regional speech by itself resolve the problem. Smith’s account of vernacular democratisation begins with Welsh’s novel, and also acknowledges that vernacular writing may alienate members of the community it addresses (Smith, 2002, p. 21). In the film, a voice recognisably situated in class and place still has to be examined for what it can say. Being allowed to speak in one’s own accent does not supply a language outside the relations one is speaking against.

This account of the voice reaches its limit at the drug. Governmentality is not simply a theory of language, but the evidence offered here so far is verbal: Renton can narrate a refusal through terms that also organise his compliance. His pursuit of bodily collapse raises a different question. How does an attachment become desirable when it damages the capacity to act that self-management is supposed to preserve? The withdrawal sequence gives that question a spatial form.

2. Space and the body’s investment in its own subjection

Renton’s parents lock him in his childhood bedroom to get him clean. Their plan gives the room an institutional purpose on a domestic scale: containment is to restore the body to a condition in which it can manage itself. As the room stretches, the plan remains in place while the space needed to carry it out becomes uninhabitable in another sense. The walls recede, the toy-train wallpaper extends into a tunnel, and familiar proportions cease to orient the body. The reading concerns the effect visible on screen; it does not require an unverified account of the lens or set that produced it.

Lefebvre’s conceived, perceived and lived space are useful because they keep three claims about the room from being treated as one (Lefebvre, 1991, pp. 38–39). The parents conceive a recovery space. Ordinary spatial practice depends on dimensions through which a body can move and find its bearings. Renton lives the room through withdrawal and hallucination. The sequence forces these dimensions apart: the intended use neither controls his experience nor restores the everyday geometry on which that use relies. A familiar location, ordinarily available to social realism as an explanation of its inhabitant, becomes the place where explanation loses its footing.

The room nevertheless remains socially specific. Its childhood surfaces have not disappeared: the trains, the music images and the Hibernian rosette above the bed are still there, but they no longer combine into a stable account of development. Winnicott’s transitional phenomena describe the objects and cultural practices through which an inner life acquires relations with the world (Winnicott, 1953). These surfaces can be read in that register without treating them as a clinical history of Renton’s addiction. What the film brings together is a set of attachments whose value lies in the feeling they sustain, rather than in the control they give him over the conditions of his life.

The title’s hobby supplies one such image: following a system’s movements while remaining a spectator to them. The rosette supplies another attachment, with a different history and a different social life. Neither trainspotting nor football allegiance is inherently a form of exclusion; both can organise communities and participation. Within this bedroom, however, their traces are held around an isolated body. The framing makes them available as figures of belonging that endure when practical agency has contracted. The resemblance to the later drug attachment is an interpretation of that arrangement, not proof that these pursuits caused it.

Dawn’s cry and the television hallucination bring other relations into the enclosure. The dead child returns as part of Renton’s crisis, while the imagined game show processes HIV, guilt and parental attachment into a sequence of questions and verdicts. His mother answers the demand to classify him by affirming their relation; the programme accepts that response as another correct answer. What resists the format is made usable by it. The bedroom repeats the problem of the opening catalogue, but with a body rather than a shopping list at stake.

The toilet sequence makes the attachment more difficult still. Renton retrieves the lost suppositories by going after what his body has expelled, and the film makes the descent an adventure. Lacan’s objet a identifies the place an object occupies in sustaining desire, rather than the ordinary satisfaction its acquisition ought to provide (Lacan, 1977). That distinction illuminates the disproportion between the object and the bodily cost of recovering it. It does not mean that heroin has no chemical effects, or that a psychoanalytic category explains those effects. It helps explain why the film can make an object surrounded by degradation appear as something the body must have.

Winnicott and Lacan do not thereby become one developmental theory. The former helps ask how attachments have been supported within a particular life; the latter names a structure of desire that cannot be reduced to inadequate support. The room places those questions beside each other without reconciling them. Its objects make the history of the attachment visible, but neither the objects nor the drive can explain why the destruction of shared life has become the setting in which this history is lived.

3. Reproduction and the dissolution of the collective

The opening sprint offers Edinburgh as fragments passing at the speed of the body. Those fragments locate Renton without giving the city enough duration to explain him. Harvey’s account of capital moving out of the geographies it has made supplies one historical question for that loss of legibility: what remains of a social world when investment relocates and leaves its existing arrangements damaged (Harvey, 2004, p. 66)? The cutting does not itself demonstrate that economic history. It gives a formal reading of space that the history can help situate. Capital’s withdrawal must also be distinguished from accumulation by dispossession, which concerns the appropriation of assets; they are related processes, not interchangeable names for whatever has happened to the city.

An account organised around departing investment can still leave the work of sustaining people at the edge of the picture. Federici’s history of reproductive labour asks how the maintenance of bodies and daily life becomes subordinated to accumulation while remaining necessary to it (Federici, 2004). Dawn’s death is the film’s most direct pressure on that omission. The adults have rooms, drugs and one another, but the arrangement cannot provide the care on which a child’s survival depends.

The distribution of attention within the film repeats that pressure unevenly. Renton’s bodily crisis receives the colour, movement and sound through which collapse becomes an experience for the viewer. Alison’s loss is given less space, and Dawn’s return on the ceiling takes the child into Renton’s withdrawal. The failure of care becomes visible through its catastrophe and then through the male body’s experience of that catastrophe. This is a claim about the film’s allocation of attention, not a judgement that Alison alone should have kept the collective going.

The essay is caught in that allocation too. Renton supplies the sequences through which its theories can be brought into relation, while Alison enters chiefly where his account proves insufficient. Moving attention towards care therefore requires more than adding her name to the interpretation: it requires asking what becomes an analytical object only after the work has failed. Dawn’s death brings reproductive labour into view at the moment the group can no longer perform it.

Renton’s final theft then secures an individual future by dismantling the arrangement on which it depends. The £16,000 is not primitive accumulation in Marx’s sense: stealing the proceeds of a drug deal does not establish the historical separation of producers from the means of production. The analogy concerns the form of the exit, in which one person gains a future by taking the group’s resources and leaving it to absorb the loss. That is already enough to put pressure on the opening claim of freedom. Refusing an acceptable life and acquiring one through betrayal can belong to the same logic of private survival without every transaction being made an instance of one economic concept.

4. What the argument cannot absorb

The spectator’s pleasure complicates the account of subjection. The film does not simply display the body’s damage; its cutting and music make damage available as intensity. Fisher’s capitalist realism helps ask how an environment can organise both the terms of escape and the pleasures through which those terms remain inhabitable (Fisher, 2009). The relation between Renton’s investment and the viewer’s enjoyment is nevertheless a question, not an established identity. Taking a drug and enjoying a film are different acts. Their juxtaposition matters because the film makes the consequences of one consumable through the other.

Before Renton leaves, he sets aside a share for Spud. That act requires the argument to retreat from its strongest formulation. If every remaining attachment were simply another instrument of private survival, there would be no reason to preserve Spud’s claim once the others’ claims had been cancelled. The money does not restore the collective, and it may express guilt or selective loyalty rather than a politics of solidarity. But those alternatives still require a relation to another person that the total account of self-management had made too easy to dismiss. Spud’s share limits the thesis whether or not it redeems Renton.

Endnotes poses a different limit. “Misery and Debt” describes the production of surplus populations alongside accumulation, but it does not say that people no longer needed as labour are left outside administration. Its conclusion insists on their continued management, including through punitive institutions (Endnotes, 2010). The pressure on this essay therefore concerns the purpose of management: a population can be administered without being cultivated as the productive, self-managing subject through whom the earlier argument understood power to operate.

The distinction changes the question asked of the bedroom. Parents trying to restore a son, services processing a dependent body and a labour market with diminished use for that body need not form a single apparatus with one intention. Their actions can intersect while their ends diverge. The film does not establish which relation has priority, and a framework that treats every intervention as the reproduction of useful labour would conceal the very possibility Endnotes identifies: management continuing where absorption into stable waged employment has failed.

Renton’s final walk consequently cannot be read as proof that the system has ceased to watch him, any more than Spud’s money can prove that solidarity has survived intact. What the film gives the argument is a compromised exit, made from betrayal and a residual obligation together. The task is to retain both without allowing either to finish the other’s meaning.
